The International Baccalaureate Diploma Programme has a reputation for being difficult. That reputation is deserved. Over two years, you study six subjects, complete many different forms of assessment, write an Extended Essay, take Theory of Knowledge, and meet the Creativity, Activity, Service requirement. There is rarely just one deadline to think about.

And yet, having completed the IB with 44 out of 45 points, I think it was worth it—not only because of the final result, but because of the habits the programme forced me to develop along the way.

## It is more than six final exams

In the IB, every subject is graded from 1 to 7. Six subjects therefore provide up to 42 points, while Theory of Knowledge and the Extended Essay can contribute another three, for a maximum total of 45.

That simple scoring system hides how varied the work is. A course grade can combine several written examinations with oral assessments, laboratory reports, essays, presentations, or an independent investigation. The exact balance differs by subject, but the principle is consistent: doing well means demonstrating your understanding in more than one format.

My Higher Level subjects were Mathematics: Analysis and Approaches, English B, and Biology. At Standard Level, I took Chemistry, Polish A, and History. This combination meant moving between mathematical problem-solving, scientific experiments, literary analysis, language, and historical argument. It was demanding, but it also made my education broader than it would have been if I had specialised early.

## You learn to manage long projects

The Extended Essay is probably the clearest example of what makes the IB different. It asks you to define a focused question, find and assess sources, develop an argument, and sustain a substantial piece of independent work over time.

Individual subjects have smaller research projects of their own. These projects often begin while regular classes and tests continue, so progress cannot be left until the final week. I had to learn how to divide an ambiguous task into smaller steps, decide what mattered, and keep working without someone specifying every next action.

That experience resembles university much more closely than preparing for a single exam does. At university, the difficult part is often not understanding an isolated concept. It is balancing several courses, projects, and deadlines while deciding independently where to put your attention. The IB gave me an early version of that challenge.

## Breadth becomes a practical advantage

It is easy to see an unrelated subject as time taken away from the field you care about most. I was strongly interested in mathematics and science, but the IB did not let me study only those areas.

In retrospect, that breadth was useful. Writing essays taught me to make a claim precise and support it. Languages made me more attentive to how ideas are communicated. History required comparing evidence and recognising that the same facts can support competing interpretations. Biology and Chemistry connected abstract models to experiments and imperfect measurements.

These are not separate skills that stay inside school subjects. Technical work also requires clear writing, judgement about evidence, and the ability to explain an idea to someone with a different background.

## Theory of Knowledge rewards better questions

Theory of Knowledge can initially feel unusually abstract. Instead of teaching another body of facts, it asks how knowledge is produced, justified, and limited across different fields.

Its value is not that every classroom discussion produces a final answer. The value is learning to notice assumptions: What counts as evidence here? Why do we trust this method? How much does the conclusion depend on the language or model used to express it?

Those questions are useful well beyond the IB. They encourage a kind of intellectual caution that is especially important whenever a result looks authoritative simply because it contains numbers, a graph, or a confident explanation.

## The difficulty is real

None of this means that the programme is the right choice for everyone. The workload can be intense, and the number of parallel assessments sometimes makes the experience feel like an exercise in scheduling as much as learning. The benefit also depends on the support available at a particular school and on whether the student is comfortable with a broad curriculum.

The IB is not worth choosing merely because it sounds prestigious. It is worth choosing if you want an education that combines academic range with sustained independent work, and if you are willing to become more organised when motivation alone is not enough.

## What stayed with me

My final score mattered to me, but it is not the main reason I value the IB now. The more lasting result was confidence that I could approach unfamiliar material, manage several forms of work at once, and produce something substantial without being given a complete recipe.

The programme made the transition to university less abrupt. University was still harder and more independent, but the basic rhythm was familiar: learn quickly, ask good questions, plan work over weeks rather than days, and communicate what you understand.

That is why I think the IB Diploma Programme is worth it. It does not simply test what you know at the end of high school. At its best, it changes how you learn.
